2.2 · Be Clear, Be Direct: The Golden Rules

The highest-leverage wording habits in one lesson: say what you want, precisely, positively, and stop hedging.

In one breath: Claude does its best work when you state exactly what you want (not vague vibes), quantify instead of using adjectives, phrase rules as do-this rather than don't-do-that where possible, and write like you're briefing a brilliant new colleague.

The brilliant-new-colleague test

The single best mental frame: write every prompt as if briefing a brilliant colleague on their first day. Brilliant — so you don't explain what a spreadsheet is. First day — so they know nothing about your company, your client, your standards, or what «the usual way» means. Read your prompt and ask: could a stranger execute this without asking me a question? If they'd have to ask «for whom?», «how long?», «what tone?», «what do I do with edge cases?» — the answers belong in the prompt.

The five golden rules

1. Name the deliverable, not the topic. «Something about our refund policy» → «a 150-word customer-facing paragraph explaining our 30-day refund policy, warm but firm.» Topic-prompts get essays; deliverable-prompts get artifacts. 2. Numbers beat adjectives. «Short» means nothing; «under 100 words» means one thing. «A few options» → «exactly 5 options». «Make it better» → «cut 30%, simplify vocabulary to a 12-year-old's level, keep all facts.» Every vague adjective is a dice roll. 3. Prefer positive instructions. «Don't be formal» leaves infinite wrong directions; «write like texting a smart friend» names the target. Keep necessary hard prohibitions (legal, safety, brand) as explicit don'ts — but steer with do's. 4. Say what to do with the tricky cases. Real tasks have edge cases: «If a review mentions both praise and a complaint, classify by the complaint. If it's spam, label SPAM. If you can't tell, label UNCLEAR — never guess.» That last clause is a hallucination defense from 1.6 wearing work clothes. 5. State the purpose. One line of why lets Claude make a hundred micro-decisions correctly: «This summary is for the CEO who has 60 seconds» produces a different — and correctly different — document than «this is for the engineering team's archive.»

Hedging: the silent prompt-killer

Politeness costs nothing, but hedged requests cost quality. «Maybe you could possibly suggest a few small ideas if that's okay?» models low-confidence writing, and prediction engines match register. Ask plainly: «Give me 7 ideas. Rank them by impact.» You're not being rude to anyone — you're being clear to a machine (Lesson 1.1: no feelings, no ego). Save your social-calibration energy for humans. 2026 note — instruction-following got sharp. Modern Claude models follow instructions more literally than older generations. This is wonderful — your constraints actually bind — but it means sloppy instructions also bind. If you say «list every issue», expect an exhaustive list, not the top 3 you secretly wanted. Precision is now a double-edged gift: the model does what you say, so say what you mean.

Recap

Brief Claude like a brilliant stranger: name the deliverable, quantify everything, steer with do's, legislate the edge cases, and state the purpose — modern models do what you say, so say what you mean.
