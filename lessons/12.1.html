12.1 · Micro-SaaS: Idea → Validation

Micro-SaaS: idea to validation. How to find a product worth building on the Claude API — and prove people want it BEFORE you build, so you don't waste months on something nobody buys.

In one breath: A good micro-SaaS idea is a specific, painful, narrow problem you can solve with the Claude API for a reachable audience — and the winning move is to VALIDATE demand (people will pay) before building, ideally from a service you've already delivered repeatedly.

What makes a good micro-SaaS idea

Micro-SaaS = a small, focused software product solving ONE specific problem, run by an individual or tiny team. The ingredients of a good one: A specific, painful, narrow problem. Not «an AI assistant» (too broad, competing with giants) but «a tool that turns podcast episodes into SEO show-notes» (specific, painful for a real audience, narrow enough to own). Narrow wins — you can't out-broad the big players, but you can own a niche they ignore. A reachable audience who feels the pain. Can you FIND and REACH the people with this problem (a community, a platform, a niche you know)? A great product for an unreachable audience fails. Distribution is half the battle — pick problems whose sufferers you can reach. AI does the core value. The Claude API should be doing something genuinely valuable and hard-to-replicate-manually — extraction, generation, analysis at quality (Track 9). If a simple script or a human could do it as well, it's not a strong AI product. Feasible unit economics (12.2): the value to the user must exceed your cost to serve (API + hosting) with margin. Most ideas die here if not designed for it — which is why cost engineering (9.7) is a product skill.

The best source of ideas: services you've delivered

The single best micro-SaaS ideas come from SERVICES YOU'VE ALREADY DELIVERED (Track 11) — because you've already validated that people pay for the outcome: The productize-the-service path: if you've delivered «Receipt Rescue» (11.2) to 20 accounting firms manually, you KNOW there's demand, you KNOW the exact problem, and you've already built most of the pipeline (9.3) — turning it into a self-serve product is a de-risked, validated move, not a blind bet. This is why Track 11 (services) came before Track 12 (products): services are your market research. The pain you personally feel: the second-best source — a problem YOU have that AI could solve (your capstone, 9.8, may be exactly this). You're the first user, you understand the pain intimately, and if others share it, you have a product. But VALIDATE that others share it — 'I'd use this' is not 'others will pay'. The narrow slice of a broad tool: take something people use a general AI for and make a specialised, better tool for ONE use case (a purpose-built tool beats a general chatbot for a specific job because it's tailored, guided, and reliable — your verification moat).

Validate BEFORE you build (the make-or-break step)

The most important lesson in the products path: prove people will PAY before you build. Building first is how months get wasted. Validation methods, cheapest first: Sell the service version first (Track 11): deliver the outcome manually/semi-manually to real paying clients. If they pay for the outcome delivered by hand, they'll likely pay for the automated product — and you've earned money AND validated while building nothing. The landing-page test: build a simple page describing the product and its price (a Claude-built page, 4.5/websmith), drive a little traffic (your outreach audience, 11.3), and measure interest — sign-ups, pre-orders, or even just 'notify me' clicks. Real interest before real building. The concierge MVP: offer the product's outcome but deliver it manually behind the scenes (the user thinks it's automated; you're doing it by hand with your pipeline). Validates demand and UX before you build the full self-serve automation. Talk to the audience: before building, talk to 10 people with the problem — do they feel it acutely? do they pay for solutions now? what would make them switch? Real conversations beat assumptions. The killer question: 'would you pay ₹X for this?' (and watch if they actually would, not just say yes politely). The lean principle: the goal of validation is to learn whether to build with the LEAST effort. A weekend landing-page test that saves you three months of building the wrong thing is the highest-ROI work in the whole products path. Build only what's validated; validate before you build.

Recap

A good micro-SaaS idea is a specific, narrow, painful problem for a reachable audience where AI does the core value with feasible economics — and the make-or-break discipline is to VALIDATE demand (people will pay) with the cheapest possible test before building, ideally from a service you've already sold (proven demand, built pipeline).
