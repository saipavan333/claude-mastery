12.2 · Unit Economics: API Cost vs Price

Unit economics: API cost vs price. The arithmetic that decides whether your product is a business or a money-loser — and the cost engineering (9.7) that makes thin ideas profitable.

In one breath: A product is a business only if the price a user pays exceeds your cost to serve them (API + hosting) with healthy margin — so you model unit economics before building, apply cost engineering (model mix, caching, batching from 9.7) to widen the margin, and price to the value (11.4), because many AI products die on economics that could have been designed to work.

The unit-economics equation (products edition)

Every AI product's viability reduces to per-user math: Profit per user = Price per user − Cost to serve per user where Cost to serve ≈ (API cost per user) + (hosting/infra per user) + (support/other per user) The AI-product-specific risk: unlike traditional software (where serving a user costs near-zero once built), AI products have a real MARGINAL cost per user — the API tokens they consume (9.1's per-token meter). A user who chats a lot, processes big documents, or triggers expensive operations costs you real money EVERY time. This is the trap: traditional-software intuition ('serving users is free') is wrong for AI products. Model it BEFORE building (9.7's discipline, product edition): estimate a typical user's monthly API cost (their interactions × tokens × price) and compare to your price. If cost-to-serve approaches or exceeds price, the product loses money at scale — and the fix is architectural, best designed in from the start. Watch the heavy users: a few power-users can consume most of your API cost. Model the DISTRIBUTION, not just the average — and consider usage caps or tiered pricing so heavy users pay proportionally (or you cap their cost).

Cost engineering as the margin-maker

The good news: your Track 9 economics skills (9.6, 9.7) are exactly what makes AI-product margins work. Applied to a product: Model mix (1.3, 9.7): route each operation to the cheapest capable model — Haiku for the simple bulk, Sonnet/flagship only where needed. Most product operations are simple; putting them on the cheap model can cut cost-to-serve dramatically. Caching (9.6): if your product reuses a large stable prefix (a system prompt, reference docs, examples) across many calls — and most do — caching cuts that cost ~90%. Design your prompts docs-first (3.2) so the cacheable prefix is maximal. Batching (9.6): any non-real-time processing (overnight jobs, bulk operations) gets 50% off via the Batch API. Route background work there. Prompt efficiency (2.2): fewer tokens per interaction = lower cost-to-serve. Concise prompts (2.2's brevity) are now a product-margin lever. The compounding effect: these stack (9.6/9.7) — model mix × caching × batching × prompt efficiency can turn a product that loses money per user into one with healthy margins, on the SAME features and quality. Cost engineering isn't optimization-for-later; it's what makes the product viable, best designed in.

Pricing the product (value + sustainability)

Price to the value (11.4): what's the outcome worth to the user? A tool that saves a professional hours per week can price to a fraction of that value — well above cost-to-serve. Value-based pricing (not cost-plus) is how AI products achieve strong margins. But ensure price > cost-to-serve with margin at every tier — value-pricing sets the ceiling, unit economics set the floor; your price must clear both. A product priced to value but below cost-to-serve for heavy users is still a money-loser on those users. Common models: subscription (predictable, but watch heavy users), usage-based (aligns your cost with revenue — the user who costs more pays more), tiered (free/basic/pro — the pro tier subsidises, the free tier acquires), per-outcome (pay per result — aligns perfectly with value and cost). Choose the model that aligns price with your cost-to-serve so heavy usage doesn't sink you. The free-tier trap: a generous free tier on an AI product means free users cost you real API money with no revenue — unlike traditional software's near-free free tiers. Design free tiers carefully (tight limits, or as a loss-leader you can afford) or you subsidise non-payers into bankruptcy.

Recap

A product is a business only if price-per-user exceeds cost-to-serve (API + infra) with margin across the usage distribution — so model unit economics before building, apply cost engineering (model mix, caching, batching, prompt efficiency from 9.6/9.7) to cut cost-to-serve up to ~90%, price to value with a usage-aligned model, and design free tiers carefully; many AI products die on economics that could have been designed to work.
