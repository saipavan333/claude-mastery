2.1 · The Anatomy of a Great Prompt

There is a repeatable skeleton behind every great prompt. Learn it once and you'll never stare at a blank message box again.

In one breath: Great prompts share six parts — Role, Context, Task, Constraints, Format, Examples — and while short prompts can skip parts, the skeleton tells you exactly what to add when an answer disappoints.

The six-part skeleton

Every serious prompt is built from six parts. Not every prompt needs all six — but every disappointing answer is missing at least one: Part What it does Sounds like 1. Role Sets the expertise and voice Claude adopts «You are a senior tax accountant who explains things simply…» 2. Context The background Claude cannot guess: who you are, the situation, what came before «I run a 12-person bakery in Jaipur; our margins are thin and my staff aren't tech-savvy…» 3. Task The one clear action verb — what to produce «Write a one-page staff policy on handling online orders.» 4. Constraints Rules, boundaries, must/must-nots «Under 400 words. No jargon. Don't promise refunds.» 5. Format The shape of the output «Give me: a title, 5 numbered rules, and a friendly closing line.» 6. Examples A sample of what good looks like (the heavyweight — Lesson 2.3) «Here's a policy we wrote before that has the right tone: …» Order matters less than presence — though for long prompts the strong habit is context and materials first, the task and format last, so instructions sit closest to where generation begins (the physics of Lesson 1.2 in action).

Why each part earns its place

Recall the prediction engine (1.1). Each skeleton part narrows the space of likely continuations: Role selects the register: «senior accountant» pulls precise, cautious language; without it you get generic-internet voice. Context is the part beginners omit — Claude sees only your words (1.1), so unstated background produces answers to a different, imaginary situation. Task wants one strong verb. «Help me with my resume» is a mood; «Rewrite these 3 bullet points to emphasise measurable impact» is a task. Constraints prevent the failure you'd otherwise edit out. Every «don't» you write is an edit you don't make later. Format is free money: the same content, delivered in the shape you can immediately use. Examples outperform descriptions so reliably they get their own lesson. The diagnosis habit: when an answer disappoints, don't sigh — scan the skeleton. Too generic → add Context. Wrong tone → add Role. Rambling → add Constraints + Format. Misunderstood the ask → sharpen Task. Right idea, wrong style → add an Example. Six parts, six fixes.

Recap

Six parts — Role, Context, Task, Constraints, Format, Examples — and every disappointing answer is missing a nameable one: diagnose by part, change one thing, rerun.
