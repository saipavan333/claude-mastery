1.6 · The Truth Discipline: Hallucination & Verification

Claude will sometimes be confidently wrong. Professionals aren't the people who never see hallucinations — they're the people hallucinations can't hurt.

In one breath: Because Claude composes rather than looks up, it can produce fluent falsehoods — so pros sort outputs by stakes and checkability, force sources and uncertainty into the open, and verify anything that could cost money, reputation or safety.

Why fluent falsehood is the natural failure mode

Recall Lesson 1.1: Claude composes answers from patterns; it doesn't retrieve them from a fact vault. When the pattern is strong and the training data rich, composition lands on truth with high reliability. When you ask for something sparse, specific, or after its knowledge cutoff — an exact quote, a niche statistic, a 2026 price, an obscure paper — the machinery still produces something shaped exactly like a right answer . That's a hallucination : not lying (no intent), not a glitch (no error thrown) — just prediction doing its best without ground truth. Two corollaries every operator internalises: Confidence is styling, not evidence. The tone of an answer tells you nothing about its truth. Risk concentrates predictably: precise facts, numbers, quotes, citations, names, anything recent, and anything at the edge of documented knowledge. Reasoning over material you supplied is far safer ground.

The triage: sort before you verify

Verifying everything is as amateur as verifying nothing. Sort every output in two seconds by two questions — what does being wrong cost? and can I check it? Green — creative & subjective: brainstorms, drafts, rewrites, explanations of concepts you can sanity-check. Wrongness is cheap and visible. Use freely. Yellow — factual but low-stakes: background info, summaries of supplied documents, how-to guidance. Spot-check the load-bearing claims. Red — factual and consequential: anything you'll publish, send a client, spend money on, cite, or act on medically/legally/financially. Every load-bearing fact gets verified against a primary source. No exceptions, no matter how confident the prose.

The five defenses

Habits that make hallucinations survivable — you'll deepen each in Track 3: 1. Give it an out. Add: «If you're not sure, say so — I'd rather have honest uncertainty than a guess.» Permission to admit ignorance measurably reduces invention. 2. Make it search. For anything recent or specific: «Search the web and cite your sources.» Claude with live sources ≫ Claude from memory. Then click the important links — checking citations beats trusting them. 3. Ground it in your documents. «Answer ONLY from the attached file; quote the relevant passage for each claim.» Supplied-text answers are checkable in seconds via the quotes. 4. Ask for the confidence map. «Now mark each claim: certain / likely / should-verify.» Claude is decently calibrated about its own shaky ground — the map tells you where to aim your checking minutes. 5. Second pass, fresh eyes. For red-zone work: open a NEW chat, paste the output, and ask Claude to fact-check it hostile-reviewer style. A fresh context isn't anchored to defend its earlier draft. (This «generator vs critic» split becomes an engineering pattern in Tracks 3 and 10.)

Recap

Triage by stakes and checkability; force sources, quotes and confidence into the open; verify every red-zone fact against a primary source — generation is Claude's job, verification is yours.
